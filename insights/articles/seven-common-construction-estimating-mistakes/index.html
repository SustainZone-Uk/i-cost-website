Construction Estimating & BOQ

Seven Common Construction Estimating Mistakes (And How to Avoid Them)

The most costly errors estimators make under tender pressure—from confusing markup and margin to unindexed supplier rates and missing preliminaries.

By I-Cost Editorial Team • Published 2026-10-18 • 8 min read

Why Tenders Go Wrong Under Commercial Pressure

Estimating is the commercial frontline of contracting. Estimators operate under intense deadline pressure, often receiving hundreds of revised drawings, addenda, and specifications within 48 hours of tender closing. In this fast-paced environment, even experienced commercial professionals fall into avoidable traps that silently destroy project profitability.

Here are the seven most common estimating mistakes identified on UK contracting tenders and the practical countermeasures commercial managers should implement.

1. Confusing Markup and Margin

Confusing markup with margin is one of the most widespread arithmetic errors in the construction industry. While the terms are frequently used interchangeably in conversation, their financial mechanics are fundamentally different:

  • Markup: Profit expressed as a percentage of cost: (Selling Price - Cost) / Cost × 100
  • Margin: Profit expressed as a percentage of the final selling price: (Selling Price - Cost) / Selling Price × 100

If a tender has a net direct cost of £500,000 and the company board targets a 20% gross profit margin, applying a 20% markup results in a tender price of £600,000. However, £100,000 profit on £600,000 revenue represents a margin of only 16.67%. The contractor has unintentionally left £25,000 of anticipated margin on the table before striking a single blow on site.

2. Copying Historic Rates Without Price Verification

Under tight deadlines, the temptation to copy rate build-ups from an estimate completed six months ago is enormous. However, UK construction material prices continue to adjust across key sectors. Copying an unverified brick, timber, or steel price without obtaining refreshed supplier quotes transfers market inflation risk directly onto the contractor's bottom line.

3. Pricing Preliminaries as a Flat Rule-of-Thumb Percentage

Applying an arbitrary "10% for preliminaries" across all tenders is a recipe for commercial distress. A £1m project executed over 16 weeks in an open greenfield site has vastly different preliminary overheads than an identical £1m fit-out in central London spanning 36 weeks with restricted access, no on-site storage, and strict noise curfews. Preliminaries must always be calculated against a resource-loaded programme.

4. Overlooking Waste, Laps, and Bulking Factors

Quantities shown in a bill of quantities represent net in-place measurements. They do not include material cutting waste, lap allowances, or excavation bulking:

Material / Operation BOQ Measurement Realistic Site Waste / Overlap Allowance
Facing Bricks & Blocks Net wall area 5% to 8% for cuts, breakage, and handling waste
Roofing Felt & DPM Membranes Net surface area covered 10% to 15% for side and end laps and perimeter upstands
Structural Timber & Studwork Net measured length/area 7% to 10% for cross-cutting and offcuts
Clay Soil Excavation to Spoil Net bank volume in ground 25% to 35% bulking factor when loaded into muck-away wagons

5. Missing Site Logistics and Unloading Costs

Assuming suppliers will offload materials exactly where operatives require them is a frequent mistake. If road closures, craneage hire, banksman supervision, or manual handling through pedestrian zones are required, these costs must be priced within preliminaries. Failing to identify access restrictions leads to costly site delays.

6. Ignoring Payment Terms and Retention Drag

Standard UK subcontracts often feature 30 to 45-day payment cycles and 3% to 5% retention held for 12 to 24 months until the certificate of making good defects. Contractors who fail to model the cost of funding working capital and finance interest will find cash flow severely constrained even on technically profitable contracts.

7. Submitting Without Clear Qualifications and Exclusions

When tender documents are ambiguous or incomplete, pricing on unwritten assumptions without submitting a formal qualification letter invites dispute. Every tender must clearly specify working hour limits, design boundaries, access assumptions, and exclusions (e.g., builder's work in connection with services).

Key Takeaways

  • Protect Margin from Day One: Margin leakage occurs incrementally; establish committed cost controls before placing orders.
  • Enforce Written Protocols: Verbal site requests carry 100% contractor risk. Require written confirmations of verbal instructions (CAIs) immediately.
  • Bridge the Estimating-Buying Gap: Ensure the tender estimate baseline directly governs purchase order authorization and live project tracking.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the mathematical difference between margin and markup?

Markup is profit expressed as a percentage of the cost price. Margin is profit expressed as a percentage of the selling price. Adding 20% markup to a £100 cost produces £120; achieving a 20% margin requires a selling price of £125 (£100 / (1 - 0.20)).

How should contractors price preliminaries accurately?

Preliminaries should never be priced as an arbitrary percentage of measured works. Instead, build up preliminary costs resource by resource against a realistic project programme, accounting for staff, welfare, plant hire duration, and site logistics.

Why is it risky to copy rates from previous tenders?

Material and plant prices in the UK fluctuate constantly. Historical rates do not reflect current supplier price increases, transport surcharges, updated wage agreements (e.g., CIJC or BESA rates), or site-specific delivery restrictions.
